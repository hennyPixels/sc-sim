# The Feast of Khazad-dûm

## A Chronicle of the Ages

### Part III: The Dawn of Ages

*Being an Account of the Morning after the Feast, the Preparations for
the Coming War, and the Hope that Sustains a People in the Face of Darkness,
wherein the Chronicle reaches its Conclusion*

---

# Book Three: What Endures

## Chapter XIX

The morning after the feast dawned—insofar as anything could be said to dawn in the eternal darkness of Khazad-dûm—with the ringing of the great bell in the Tower of the Watch. Six strokes, echoing through stone corridors that had channeled the sound for twenty generations, announcing the beginning of a new day.

In the guest quarters of the First Deep, Lord Náin Fireforge woke to find his wife already dressed and working at a portable desk she had set up beneath the glow-lamps.

"Dís," he said, his voice rough with sleep and the remnants of last night's ale. "Did you sleep at all?"

Lady Dís looked up from her papers, her expression distracted. "An hour. Perhaps two. I had an insight about the caching implementation—the eviction policy I proposed to Stonehammer. There's an edge case I hadn't considered, where repeated access to the same item within a short window could—"

"Dís."

She stopped, recognizing the tone in her husband's voice.

"It's the morning after the greatest feast in living memory," Náin said gently. "The edge cases can wait."

For a moment, Lady Dís seemed about to argue. Then her shoulders relaxed, and she set down her pen.

"You're right. Of course you're right." She moved to sit beside him on the edge of the bed. "But it was such a good feast, wasn't it? The King's speech. The songs. That old cable-layer—Bofur—standing up and claiming his share of the glory."

"It was a good feast," Náin agreed. "One for the chronicles."

They sat in comfortable silence for a moment, two dwarves who had shared three centuries of partnership—in marriage, in work, in the raising of three sons who had inherited their parents' talents and temperaments in varying measures.

"The King was right to warn us about Morgoth," Lady Dís said eventually. "The shadow grows. Even in the Eastern Deeps, we've heard reports. Orc scouts spotted near the outer gates. Strange lights in the mountains at night. The Elves say something stirs in Angband, something terrible."

"The Elves always say something stirs in Angband. It's been stirring for centuries."

"This is different. I spoke with an Elven merchant last month—he was passing through with a caravan of trade goods from Doriath. He said their king, Thingol, has doubled the guard on the Girdle of Melian. He said the Noldor are forging weapons at a rate not seen since the War of Wrath. Something is coming, Náin. Something that will test everything we've built."

Náin was silent for a long moment.

"Then it's good we built it," he said at last. "Whatever comes, we're more prepared than we've ever been. The network connects us. The PartsDB tracks our resources. The power grid keeps our forges running day and night. If Morgoth wants to test us, let him come. He'll find dwarves ready to fight."

Lady Dís leaned her head against his shoulder.

"I love you, you know. Even when you're being absurdly optimistic."

"Someone has to balance your relentless realism."

---

## Chapter XX

Across the First Deep, in the modest quarters assigned to the Engineering Corps, Kira Deepdelver was also awake—though in her case, she had not slept at all.

She stood at the window of her chamber, looking out over the vast cavern that housed the central hub of the telegraph network. From here, she could see the main switching station—a building of stone and bronze that processed ten thousand messages a day, routing each one to its proper destination with mechanical precision. She could see the cable conduits that radiated outward from the station like spokes from a wheel, carrying copper wire to every corner of the realm. She could see the power lines that fed the system, connecting it to the hydroelectric generators in the Second Deep.

She had designed much of this. Not alone—no one could build something so vast alone—but she had been there from the beginning, had shaped the architecture that others had implemented, had solved the problems that had threatened to derail the project at every turn.

And now it was finished.

The realization sat strangely with her. For fifteen years, the network had been her purpose, her passion, the thing that got her out of bed each morning and kept her working late into each night. Now that it was complete, now that it hummed with the traffic of a thriving realm, she found herself wondering: what next?

A knock at the door interrupted her thoughts.

"Come in."

The door opened to reveal Thorin Stonehammer, looking remarkably alert for someone who had been drinking ale until the small hours of the morning. He carried a rolled scroll in one hand and a cup of hot mushroom tea in the other.

"Couldn't sleep either?" he asked.

"Too much to think about."

Thorin nodded, understanding. He had felt the same way when the PartsDB project had been completed, decades earlier. The emptiness that followed achievement. The question of what to do with a life that had been defined by a single goal.

"I brought you something," he said, handing her the scroll. "Lady Dís's proposal for the caching layer. She revised it this morning—apparently she realized there was an edge case she'd missed."

Kira accepted the scroll, unrolling it to scan the contents. The revision was elegant—a simple modification to the eviction policy that addressed the edge case without adding significant complexity.

"This is good work," she said.

"She's a remarkable dwarf. Her sons are impressive too—I spoke with the youngest, Dáin, at the feast. He has ideas about extending the network to the settlements beyond the gates. Claims it's technically feasible."

"It is feasible," Kira said. "I've considered it myself. The challenge isn't technical—it's political. The outer settlements are nominally independent. Convincing them to join our network would require negotiations, treaties, probably concessions we're not prepared to make."

"The King might feel differently, given what's coming."

Kira looked up sharply. "You believe the warnings?"

"I believe something is changing. The scouts have been reporting increased orc activity for months. The trading caravans from the East have been arriving with tales of darkness spreading, of creatures emerging from Angband that haven't been seen since the First War. Whether it's Morgoth himself or just another of his periodic provocations, I can't say. But I think we'd be fools to ignore the signs."

He moved to stand beside her at the window, looking out at the network hub.

"This is what will save us," he said quietly. "Not walls or weapons, though those matter too. Communication. Coordination. The ability to respond to threats in minutes rather than days. You've given us a fighting chance, Kira. Whatever comes next, we have a fighting chance."

---

## Chapter XXI

The hours after the feast were a time of departures and farewells.

One by one, the Great Houses assembled their retinues and prepared for the journey back to their home domains. Lord Náin Fireforge was among the first to leave, his caravan departing via the Eastern Tunnel Road with an escort of forty guards—not because the tunnels were dangerous, but because protocol demanded that a Lord of his stature travel with appropriate ceremony.

Before he left, he sought out Thorin Stonehammer for a final conversation.

"The caching proposal," Náin said, clasping Thorin's forearm in farewell. "My wife wants to oversee the implementation personally. She'll return in a month with a team of engineers."

"I'll have quarters prepared. And access to the PartsDB development environment."

"She'll appreciate that. Fair warning: once she starts, she won't stop until it's perfect. Our household budget may need to adjust for extended accommodation."

Thorin smiled. "Perfection is what we do. She'll be in good company."

Lord Grór Ironfoot departed next, his sedan chair carried with elaborate care through the tunnels toward the Northern Reaches. Despite his age, his mind remained sharp, and he spent the first hour of the journey dictating notes to a scribe—observations about the feast, ideas for improvements to the railway system, political assessments of the other Lords' speeches.

"The King spoke well," he dictated. "His warning about Morgoth was timely, though I wonder if the younger dwarves truly understand what we face. They've never known war. Not real war—the kind that threatens the survival of nations. They think in terms of skirmishes and raids, not the total conflict that consumed the world before their birth."

He paused, marshaling his thoughts.

"We must prepare them. Not with words, but with training. The telegraph network is a weapon—the King was right about that—but weapons are useless in unskilled hands. We need drills. Exercises. Simulations of crisis conditions. When the darkness comes—and it will come—our people must know how to use the tools we've given them."

The scribe wrote furiously, struggling to keep up with the old Lord's racing thoughts.

"Add a note at the end," Grór concluded. "Remind me to propose this to the King at the next council meeting. 'Crisis preparedness initiative.' That should be the title. Make it sound official."

---

## Chapter XXII

Lord Balin Goldvein was the last of the Great Houses to depart, in part because his retinue was the largest and in part because he insisted on personally thanking every cook, servant, and attendant who had contributed to the feast's success.

"Hospitality is a sacred duty," he explained to Thorin Stonehammer, who had been pressed into service as an escort while Balin made his rounds. "These dwarves worked through the night to ensure we were fed, watered, and entertained. The least I can do is acknowledge their efforts."

"You've been thanking them for three hours," Thorin observed.

"And I'll continue for three more if necessary. Gratitude costs nothing but time, and I have time in abundance."

It was, Thorin reflected, a philosophy that explained much about Lord Goldvein's reputation. He was not the wealthiest of the Lords, despite his house's name. He was not the most powerful, or the most influential, or the most technically accomplished. But he was the most beloved—a dwarf who remembered the names of servants, who sent gifts to the families of workers who had served him well, who treated every interaction as an opportunity to build connection.

In a realm that would soon face existential threat, such connections might prove more valuable than gold.

Finally, after thanking the last baker's apprentice for "those delightful honeycakes—my grandmother used to make ones just like them, you know," Lord Goldvein allowed himself to be escorted to the departure platform, where his train—for he had insisted on traveling by rail despite the longer journey—waited with steam already rising from its engine.

"Stonehammer," he said, clasping Thorin's hand. "You've done remarkable work. The PartsDB, the telegraph network, all of it. When the histories are written, your name will feature prominently."

"I had help."

"We all have help. That's rather the point of civilization, isn't it? No dwarf is an island. We build on what others have built, contribute what we can, and pass the tools to the next generation." He squeezed Thorin's hand before releasing it. "Keep building, Master Archivist. Keep building until there's nothing left to build. And then start improving what you've already built."

"Sound advice."

"I'm full of sound advice. Ask anyone." Lord Goldvein winked, then turned and boarded his train with a sprightliness that belied his age. Moments later, the engine huffed, the wheels began to turn, and the Lord of the Western Mines departed for home.

---

## Chapter XXIII

With the Great Houses departed, the First Deep slowly returned to its normal rhythm. The Feasting Hall was cleaned, the decorations taken down, the tables restored to their usual austere arrangement. The guest quarters were aired and scrubbed. The kitchen staff, who had worked around the clock for a week, were finally allowed to rest.

But in the Third Deep, in the chambers that housed the PartsDB, work continued.

Thorin Stonehammer stood before the main terminal—a mechanical marvel of gears and levers and punched cards that served as the primary interface to the database—and reviewed the morning's query logs. The traffic was already picking up, he noted. Lords returning to their domains were placing orders for supplies. Engineers were checking the status of components they would need for ongoing projects. The system hummed with activity, processing dozens of requests per minute with perfect reliability.

Perfect reliability. That had been the goal from the beginning. Not ninety-nine percent uptime, not "usually works," but perfect, absolute, unshakeable reliability. Every query answered. Every update recorded. Every transaction guaranteed.

They had achieved it. Or as close to it as was possible in a world of imperfect materials and fallible maintainers. The PartsDB had been running continuously for thirty-seven years, and in that time, it had experienced exactly zero unplanned outages. Scheduled maintenance, yes. Upgrades and improvements, certainly. But never a failure. Never a moment when a dwarf in need of information had been told "the system is down."

This was what they had built. This was what the feast had celebrated. Not just the telegraph network, not just the power grid, but the entire infrastructure of reliability that undergirded Dwarven civilization. The assurance that when you needed something, it would be there. That when you sent a message, it would arrive. That when you queried the database, you would get an answer.

Thorin allowed himself a moment of quiet pride. Then he turned back to his work.

There was always more work to be done.

---

## Chapter XXIV

In the weeks that followed the feast, life in Khazad-dûm settled into new patterns.

Lady Dís Fireforge returned as promised, bringing a team of twelve engineers and enough supplies for a six-month stay. She was given a suite of offices adjacent to the PartsDB development laboratory, and within days, the clatter of mechanical calculators and the scratching of pens on paper announced that work had begun on the caching layer.

"The basic structure is straightforward," she explained to Thorin at their first progress meeting. "A hash map for the cache itself, with the part number as key and the full record as value. A doubly-linked list to track access order for LRU eviction. The integration points with the existing B-tree index are clean—we intercept queries at the API layer, check the cache first, and only fall through to the index on a miss."

"What about consistency?" Thorin asked. "If a record is updated while it's in the cache—"

"Write-through policy. Every update goes to both the cache and the main index simultaneously. Cache entries are invalidated on update, forcing a fresh read on the next access. It adds a small amount of latency to writes, but writes are rare compared to reads, and the consistency guarantee is worth the cost."

Thorin nodded approvingly. It was exactly the approach he would have taken.

Meanwhile, in the Northern Reaches, Lord Grór Ironfoot was implementing his crisis preparedness initiative. Twice a week, the telegraph operators in his domain were required to participate in drills—simulated emergencies that tested their ability to handle high-priority traffic, route around failed stations, and maintain composure under pressure.

The first drill was a disaster. Operators fumbled their keys, messages were garbled, priority routing failed because someone had forgotten to configure the backup paths. Lord Grór, observing from his command post, was unsurprised.

"This is why we drill," he told his staff. "To fail safely, where the cost is embarrassment rather than lives. Note every error. Document every procedure that failed. Then fix them, and drill again."

By the third drill, performance had improved significantly. By the tenth, the operators were handling simulated crises with something approaching confidence. Lord Grór began planning more complex scenarios—multi-point failures, coordinated attacks, situations that would require judgment calls rather than simple procedure-following.

"When the real crisis comes," he said, "they'll be ready. Or as ready as anyone can be for the unknown."

---

## Chapter XXV

In the Eastern Deeps, young Dáin Fireforge—son of Lord Náin and Lady Dís, barely seventy years old and already showing the intellectual gifts that characterized his family—was pursuing his vision of an extended network.

The outer settlements were a patchwork of small communities, each fiercely independent, each suspicious of the centralized power represented by Khazad-dûm. They had their own traditions, their own dialects, their own ways of doing things. Convincing them to join the telegraph network would require patience, diplomacy, and a deep understanding of their concerns.

Dáin had all three.

He began with the settlement of Azanulbizar, a mining community nestled in a valley just outside the eastern gates. It was the largest of the outer settlements, home to nearly three thousand dwarves, and its cooperation would set the tone for negotiations with the others.

"We don't need your network," the settlement's elder, a gruff dwarf named Thráin, told Dáin at their first meeting. "We've done fine without it for five hundred years. Why should we change now?"

"Because the world is changing," Dáin replied. "Because Morgoth's shadow grows, and when it falls, isolation will be a weakness rather than a strength. Because the network offers benefits that outweigh its costs—instant communication with Khazad-dûm, access to the markets of the Seven Deeps, the ability to call for help in emergencies."

"And the costs?"

"A one-time installation fee, covered by a loan from the Goldvein Treasury at favorable rates. Ongoing maintenance, which we'll train your own people to perform. A small contribution to the general operating fund—perhaps ten gold pieces per year, less than you spend on candles."

Thráin stroked his beard, considering.

"And what about control? If we join your network, do we answer to Khazad-dûm? Do we lose our independence?"

"You retain full autonomy. The network is a tool, not a government. You can use it or not, as you see fit. We ask only that you maintain the equipment in good working order and refrain from transmitting false information that might endanger other users."

The negotiations continued for three weeks. Dáin made concessions where he could, held firm where he had to, and slowly built a rapport with the suspicious elder. Finally, Thráin agreed to a trial period: six months of network access, after which Azanulbizar would decide whether to join permanently.

It was a beginning.

---

## Chapter XXVI

Six months became a year. A year became a decade.

The caching layer was completed, tested, and deployed. Lady Dís's predictions proved accurate: query times dropped by thirty-two percent, and user satisfaction—measured by a survey distributed through the telegraph network itself—rose significantly.

The crisis drills in the Northern Reaches expanded to the entire realm. Once a month, a coordinated exercise tested the network's ability to handle emergency traffic, and each exercise revealed new weaknesses to be addressed and new strengths to be cultivated.

The outer settlements, one by one, joined the network. Azanulbizar was followed by Zirak-zigil, then Barazinbar, then a dozen smaller communities whose names were known only to their inhabitants. By the end of the decade, the telegraph network had extended beyond the gates of Khazad-dûm to encompass a constellation of allied settlements stretching across the mountains.

And through it all, the shadow grew.

The reports that had been rumors became confirmed intelligence. Morgoth was massing his forces. Balrogs, long thought to be creatures of legend, were sighted leading orc armies through the passes of the north. Dragons—actual dragons, breathing fire and speaking with cunning voices—emerged from Angband to terrorize the lands of Men and Elves.

The free peoples of Middle-earth prepared for war. The Elves forged weapons and fortified their strongholds. The Men trained their young and made alliances with their neighbors. And the Dwarves—the Dwarves continued to build.

Not walls, though they built those too. Not weapons, though the forges of the Seven Deeps worked day and night to produce arms and armor. They built something more subtle, more profound: the infrastructure of coordination. The telegraph network became the nervous system of a defense that stretched across hundreds of miles of mountainous terrain. The PartsDB tracked not just inventory but logistics—where supplies were needed, where they could be sourced, how quickly they could be transported.

When the first orc raid struck the settlement of Zirak-zigil, the defenders were ready. The telegraph had carried warnings hours before the attack, giving the inhabitants time to evacuate to fortified positions. The relief force from Khazad-dûm, dispatched via the railway system, arrived before the orcs could establish a siege. The battle was short and decisive: three hundred orcs killed, with only a dozen Dwarven casualties.

It was, the chroniclers would later note, the first test of the new systems in actual combat.

It would not be the last.

---

## Chapter XXVII

Durin VI did not live to see the final victory.

The King who had authorized the telegraph network, who had stood in the Feasting Hall and warned of the coming darkness, fell in the seventy-third year of the conflict, struck down by an orc's poisoned blade while inspecting the front lines. He was six hundred and forty-two years old, still vigorous, still sharp of mind, still the embodiment of Dwarven endurance.

His funeral was the largest in the history of Khazad-dûm.

The telegraph network—his network, the network he had championed and funded and blessed—carried the news to every corner of the realm within the hour. By nightfall, every settlement under the mountain and beyond knew of the King's passing. By the next morning, tributes were pouring in from allies across Middle-earth: Elven songs of mourning, Mannish declarations of respect, and messages from Dwarven communities in distant mountains who had never met Durin VI but who honored his legacy nonetheless.

His son, Náin I, ascended to the throne.

"My father built for the future," the new King declared in his coronation address. "He saw not just what was, but what could be. The network that connects us, the systems that sustain us, the traditions that unite us—these are his legacy. I will honor that legacy by continuing to build. By continuing to improve. By continuing to prepare for whatever challenges lie ahead."

The crowd cheered, but the cheers were tempered by grief.

And in the depths of the Third Deep, Thorin Stonehammer—now ancient himself, four hundred and twelve years old, the last surviving member of the original PartsDB design team—listened to the coronation through the telegraph, and wept.

---

## Chapter XXVIII

The war continued for another century.

It was not constant—there were periods of relative peace, when Morgoth's attention turned elsewhere and the Dwarves could catch their breath—but it was unrelenting. Each year brought new challenges, new enemies, new demands on the infrastructure that Thorin and Kira and the Lady Dís and countless others had built.

And each year, that infrastructure proved its worth.

The telegraph network evolved. New protocols were developed, allowing for encrypted military communications that even Morgoth's spies could not decipher. The PartsDB expanded to include a real-time logistics module, tracking the movement of supplies across the front with minute-by-minute updates. The power grid was hardened against attack, with redundant generators and backup cables that ensured the lights never went dark.

The Dwarves adapted. The younger generations, raised in the shadow of war, took the network for granted—they could not imagine a time when messages took weeks to travel between the Deeps. The older generations, who remembered the before-times, marveled at how quickly the extraordinary had become ordinary.

And slowly, painfully, at a cost in lives that the chroniclers would later struggle to calculate, the tide began to turn.

Morgoth's forces were driven back. The outer settlements were secured. The passes through the mountains were fortified to the point where no army, however large, could hope to breach them. The Dwarves, united by their network and sustained by their infrastructure, had achieved what many had thought impossible: they had survived.

---

## Chapter XXIX

Kira Deepdelver died in the one hundred and seventy-eighth year of the war, at the age of three hundred and twelve.

She had continued to work until her final weeks, refining protocols and training the next generation of network engineers. Her last project was the implementation of the Palantír Protocol on a realm-wide scale—the image transmission system that she had kept secret for so long, now deployed to every major station in the network.

The first image transmitted under her supervision was a portrait of Durin VI, rendered in dots and dashes that the receiving station reassembled into a recognizable likeness. It took four hours to transmit and another two to render, but when it was complete—when the King's face appeared on the slate screen of the receiving station, clear enough to recognize despite the low resolution—the observers wept with wonder.

"This is what we dreamed of," Kira said, her voice weak but triumphant. "Communication without words. Understanding without translation. The ability to share not just information, but experience."

She died three days later, surrounded by her colleagues and students.

Her funeral, like the King's before her, was announced via the network she had built. But unlike the King's funeral, hers was attended by engineers and technicians rather than nobles and dignitaries. These were her people, the dwarves who had worked beside her for decades, who had learned from her and challenged her and been challenged in return.

They spoke of her brilliance, her determination, her unwillingness to accept "good enough" when "perfect" was within reach. They spoke of her kindness, her patience with young engineers who made mistakes, her ability to explain complex concepts in simple terms. They spoke of the network—her network—and how it had changed their world.

And then they went back to work.

Because that was what Kira would have wanted. That was what Dwarves did. They mourned, they remembered, and they continued to build.

---

## Chapter XXX

Thorin Stonehammer was the last of the founders to die.

He lived to be four hundred and eighty-nine years old—ancient even by Dwarven standards, a living link to an era that had passed into legend. In his final years, he spent his days in the Archives, surrounded by the records of everything the PartsDB had tracked and everything the network had transmitted.

Young dwarves would visit him, seeking wisdom about the old days.

"What was it like," they would ask, "before the network? Before the database? How did you manage?"

And Thorin would tell them. He would speak of runners carrying messages through dark tunnels, of scribes maintaining ledgers by hand, of the chaos that erupted whenever a shipment went missing or a forge ran out of essential supplies. He would speak of the frustration that had driven the founders to dream of something better, and the determination that had turned those dreams into reality.

"We didn't build the network because it was easy," he would say. "We built it because it was necessary. Because the alternative was disorder, and disorder was death."

The young dwarves would nod, not quite understanding. They had grown up with instant communication and reliable infrastructure. They could not truly imagine a world without it.

But Thorin could imagine it. Thorin remembered it. And in his final moments, as the light faded and his ancient heart slowed, he took comfort in the knowledge that because of what he and his colleagues had built, no dwarf would ever have to endure that chaos again.

---

## Epilogue: The Chronicle Continues

The Feast of Khazad-dûm passed into legend.

Centuries later, when the war against Morgoth was won and the Dwarves entered a new age of prosperity, the descendants of those who had celebrated that night would gather in the Feasting Hall and retell the stories. They would speak of the King's wisdom, of Lord Grór's preparation, of Lady Dís's innovations, of Kira's brilliance, of Thorin's quiet dedication. They would sing the songs that had been sung that night—"The Founding of the Mansion," "The Forging of Mithril," "The Song of the Singing Wire"—and raise their mugs in tribute to ancestors they had never known.

And they would use the network.

They would send messages to friends in distant settlements, check the PartsDB for the location of a needed component, query the logistics system for the status of a pending shipment. They would do these things without thinking, without marveling, without recognizing the miracle that their ancestors had wrought.

This was the true legacy of the feast: not the celebration itself, but what the celebration had commemorated. The infrastructure that made modern Dwarven life possible. The tools that connected a scattered people into a unified nation. The systems that had endured through war and peace, through crisis and prosperity, through the rise and fall of kings.

The chronicle continues. The network hums with the voices of new generations. The PartsDB, expanded beyond anything its founders could have imagined, tracks not just parts but people, not just inventory but history.

And in the deep places of the mountain, where the glow-lamps cast their eternal light on corridors of carved stone, the telegraph keys continue their endless song:

*Dot-dash. Dot-dash-dot. Dash-dot-dot.*

A message from the Eastern Deeps to the Western Mines.

*Dot-dot-dot. Dash-dash-dash. Dot-dot-dot.*

A call for help that will never go unanswered.

*Dash-dot. Dash. Dot-dash. Dash-dot-dot-dash.*

"Network Transmits All Safely."

The work endures.
