# The Feast of Khazad-dûm

## A Chronicle of the Ages

### Part I: The Gathering of the Houses

*Being an Account of the Great Celebration following the Completion of the
Telegraph Network and the Unification of the Dwarven Realms under the
Light of the Palantír Protocol, in the Manner of the Ancient Chronicles*

---

# Book One: The Summons

## Chapter I

In the one thousand seven hundred and forty-second year of the First Age, when the shadow of Morgoth lay heavy upon the lands of Beleriand yet had not breached the mighty gates of Khazad-dûm, there came a proclamation from the throne of Durin VI that would echo through every tunnel, every forge, and every humble dwelling carved into the living rock of the Misty Mountains.

The proclamation, etched in mithril upon tablets of obsidian and carried by runners through the newly completed telegraph lines that now connected every corner of the realm, declared that a feast would be held—a celebration the likes of which had not been seen since the founding of the great mansion itself. For the work was complete. The tunnels were carved. The telegraph sang with the voices of distant stations. And the great PartsDB, that monument to Dwarven ingenuity which catalogued every rivet, every gem, every length of mithril wire in the kingdom, hummed with perfect efficiency in the depths of the Third Deep.

Thorin Stonehammer, who served as Chief Archivist of the Parts Repository—a position of considerable honor, for it was he who maintained the sacred indices and ensured that no bolt went unaccounted, no component misplaced—stood before the great bronze doors of the Feasting Hall of Durin and permitted himself a rare smile. The smile creased a face weathered by three centuries of labor, a face that had seen the first telegraph key hammered into shape and had witnessed the final connection that united the Seven Deeps into one harmonious network.

"It is finished," he murmured, running his thick fingers along the doorframe where artisans had carved the history of their achievement in intricate relief. Here was depicted the laying of the first cable. There, the construction of the hydroelectric generators that harnessed the underground rivers. And in the center, dominating all other images, the great tree of the network itself—nodes and edges spreading like the roots of an ancient oak, connecting every station, every relay, every sounder that now carried the voice of the realm.

Behind him, the sound of approaching footsteps echoed through the corridor. Heavy boots on stone. The rhythm of a military patrol.

"Master Stonehammer."

He turned. A young dwarf in the crimson livery of the Royal Guard stood at attention, his beard still short but meticulously braided in the manner of the warrior caste.

"The first of the Great Houses has arrived. House Fireforge, from the Eastern Deeps. Lord Náin Fireforge himself leads the delegation."

Thorin nodded slowly. It had begun.

---

## Chapter II

The Eastern Deeps had always been known for their forges. While other regions of Khazad-dûm specialized in mining or gem-cutting or the delicate work of inscription, the Eastern Deeps had mastered the art of metallurgy to a degree that bordered on the mystical. It was said that Náin Fireforge could look at an ingot of raw ore and tell you not only its composition but the mountain from which it had been torn, the age of the stone that had cradled it, and the precise temperature at which it would yield its secrets.

His arrival at the Feasting Hall was appropriately dramatic.

The doors of the main corridor burst open—not violently, but with the controlled force of a master smith testing a hinge—and through them strode a dwarf who seemed carved from the same bronze as the decorative panels that lined the walls. Náin Fireforge stood four feet eleven inches tall, which was considered impressive among his people, and his beard, a cascade of silver shot through with veins of copper-red, fell to his belt where it was gathered into seven braids, each capped with a different precious metal.

Behind him came his household: his wife, the Lady Dís Fireforge, whose reputation as a mathematician rivaled her husband's fame as a metallurgist; their three sons, Thorin the Younger, Frerin, and Dáin; and a retinue of forty-seven craftsmen, engineers, and servants who had made the three-day journey through the connecting tunnels from the Eastern Deeps.

"Stonehammer!" Náin's voice boomed through the corridor with the resonance of a struck anvil. "Still standing at doors, I see. Some things never change, even when the world transforms around us."

Thorin clasped the offered forearm in the traditional greeting. "And you still make an entrance like a cave-in, Fireforge. Some things indeed remain constant."

The two dwarves had known each other for two hundred years. They had collaborated on the design of the telegraph relays—Náin providing the metallurgical expertise that allowed the copper contacts to withstand millions of cycles, Thorin developing the indexing system that ensured every relay was tracked, maintained, and replaced before failure could cascade through the network. Theirs was the friendship of craftsmen who had shared triumph and disaster in equal measure.

"My wife wishes to inspect the PartsDB before the feast," Náin said, lowering his voice. "She has theories about the indexing algorithms. Something about balanced trees and rotation factors."

Thorin raised an eyebrow. Lady Dís's theories had a way of becoming innovations. It was she who had first proposed using AVL trees for the location index, arguing that the guarantee of O(log n) access times was worth the additional complexity of maintaining balance. The implementation had taken six months and required the complete restructuring of three sub-systems, but the results had been undeniable: query times had dropped by a factor of five, and the system could now handle the inventory of an empire.

"The PartsDB is always open to those who would improve it," Thorin said. "I'll have one of my archivists escort her to the Third Deep."

"Excellent." Náin turned to survey the Feasting Hall's entrance, his practiced eye taking in the craftsmanship with the appreciation of a fellow artisan. "This is fine work. The bronze inlay alone must have taken a century."

"Eighty-seven years," Thorin corrected. "Master Gimli the Elder began the work. His grandson finished it."

"Three generations for a single doorframe." Náin shook his head, but his expression was one of satisfaction rather than dismay. "This is why we endure while other races rise and fall like flames. We think in centuries. We build for eternity."

## Chapter III

The arrival of House Fireforge was merely the first trickle of what would become a flood. Over the following three days, as the feast preparations intensified and the Feasting Hall was transformed from a solemn chamber of state into a venue for celebration, the Great Houses of Khazad-dûm converged upon the First Deep from every corner of the realm.

From the Northern Reaches came House Ironfoot, whose patriarch, Lord Grór Ironfoot, had personally overseen the construction of the railway system that now moved goods and passengers through the mountain faster than any dwarf could walk. Grór was old even by Dwarven standards—four hundred and twelve years had passed since his naming day—and he arrived carried in a sedan chair borne by eight of his descendants, but his eyes remained sharp and his mind sharper. It was Grór who had first recognized the potential of graph algorithms for optimizing the railway routes, and it was his engineers who had implemented Dijkstra's path-finding to ensure that every shipment of ore took the most efficient route to its destination.

"In my youth," Grór announced to anyone who would listen as his chair was set down in the reception hall, "a message from the Eastern Deeps to the Western Mines took three weeks to deliver. Three weeks! A runner would carry it through the tunnels, stopping to rest, to eat, to ask directions at every junction. Now? Now I send a telegram and have my answer before my tea grows cold. This is progress. This is what we have built."

Close behind House Ironfoot came the delegation from the Western Mines: House Goldvein, whose wealth was legendary even among a people who measured worth in precious metals. Lord Balin Goldvein traveled with an entourage of one hundred and twenty dwarves, including his personal chef (for he was particular about his food), his personal musician (for he believed no journey should be without song), and his personal archivist (for he could not bear to be separated from his extensive collection of geological surveys).

Balin was a plump dwarf, unusual for his kind, with a beard that had been dyed a subtle shade of gold to match his house colors. He was known for three things: his generosity, his temper, and his unwavering conviction that the bucket sort algorithm was the superior method for organizing inventory by price range.

"Hash tables!" he would exclaim to anyone who suggested otherwise. "Hash tables are all well and good for exact matches, but when you need to find all items in a price range, what then? You iterate through every bucket? Madness! Give me a bucket sort any day. O(n) average case, O(n) space, and perfect for uniformly distributed data!"

The debate between Balin and the hash table proponents had raged for decades, filling countless pages of the Proceedings of the Khazad-dûm Society for Computational Advancement. It showed no signs of resolution, but it had driven innovation on both sides, resulting in hybrid approaches that combined the best features of each method.

---

## Chapter IV

By the second day of arrivals, the First Deep had taken on the character of a great marketplace, with delegations from minor houses and craft guilds setting up temporary quarters in every available space. The corridors rang with a dozen dialects of Khuzdul, from the clipped consonants of the Northern Reaches to the musical vowels of the Southern Galleries.

Among the crowd, moving with purpose despite the chaos, was a dwarf who drew curious glances wherever she passed. Kira Deepdelver wore the simple grey robes of the Engineering Corps, but the mithril insignia pinned to her collar—a stylized representation of a binary tree—marked her as something more: Chief Architect of the Telegraph Network, the dwarf who had transformed theoretical designs into physical reality.

She was young, barely one hundred and thirty, an age at which most dwarves were still considered apprentices in their chosen crafts. But Kira had proven herself exceptional from her earliest years, demonstrating an intuitive understanding of signal propagation and network topology that had earned her promotion after promotion until she found herself, still young by any measure, responsible for the greatest engineering project in Dwarven history.

Now, as she made her way through the crowded corridors toward the Feasting Hall, she reflected on the journey that had brought her here.

It had begun, as so many things did, with a problem.

The old messenger system was failing. As Khazad-dûm had expanded, as new tunnels had been carved and new settlements established in previously unreachable corners of the mountain, the network of runners who carried messages between the Deeps had grown increasingly inadequate. Messages were delayed. Messages were lost. Messages arrived garbled, their contents corrupted by the game of whisper-down-the-lane that occurred when a single communication passed through a dozen hands.

The solution, proposed by a committee of elders after years of deliberation, was simple in concept: a telegraph network that would allow instantaneous communication between any two points in the realm. The implementation, however, was anything but simple.

Kira had been a junior engineer when the project began, assigned to the team responsible for designing the relay stations that would amplify signals as they traveled through miles of copper wire. She had quickly distinguished herself by identifying a critical flaw in the original design: the relays, as specified, would introduce a propagation delay that would accumulate over distance, eventually rendering long-range communication impractical.

"The signal degrades with each relay," she had explained to the senior engineers, sketching diagrams on a slate board. "Not the amplitude—we can boost that easily enough—but the timing. Each relay adds a small delay. Over ten relays, the delay is noticeable. Over fifty, it's significant. Over the distances we're planning, hundreds of relays, the Morse patterns will blur into meaninglessness."

The solution she proposed was elegant: predictive timing compensation, where each relay would not simply retransmit the received signal but would adjust its timing based on its position in the network, compensating for accumulated delays. It required a complete redesign of the relay hardware and a sophisticated algorithm for calculating compensation factors, but it worked.

That success had led to others. When the question of network topology arose—how should the stations be connected to ensure resilience against failures?—it was Kira who had proposed the hybrid approach that was eventually adopted: a backbone of redundant connections between major stations, with tree-structured branches extending to smaller outposts. When the power requirements of the network threatened to exceed the capacity of the existing hydroelectric generators, it was Kira who had developed the load-balancing algorithms that distributed demand across multiple power sources.

And when the project finally neared completion, after fifteen years of construction and countless setbacks, it was Kira who had personally tested the final connection, sending a message from the deepest point of the Southern Galleries to the highest watchtower of the Northern Reaches in the span of a single heartbeat.

Now, as she approached the Feasting Hall, she allowed herself a moment of satisfaction. The work was done. The network was complete. And tonight, she would celebrate with the dwarves who had made it possible.

## Chapter V

The Feasting Hall of Durin was vast beyond imagining.

Carved from the living rock of the mountain's heart over a period of three centuries, it could accommodate ten thousand dwarves seated at tables, with room for another five thousand standing along the walls. The ceiling, lost in shadow despite the thousands of lanterns that lined the walls, was said to reach a height of two hundred feet at its apex, though no one had ever verified this claim. Great pillars, each carved from a single block of granite and decorated with the deeds of ancient heroes, marched in orderly rows from the entrance to the far wall, where the King's Table stood on a raised dais.

On the evening of the feast, the hall was transformed.

Banners in the colors of every Great House hung from the pillars: the crimson and gold of Fireforge, the silver and blue of Ironfoot, the deep amber of Goldvein, and dozens more besides. The tables, normally plain stone slabs suitable for everyday meals, had been covered with cloths of finest linen and set with plates of hammered silver and goblets of gold. At the center of each table stood a miniature replica of the telegraph network, rendered in copper wire and tiny gemstones that pulsed with inner light—a gift from the Engineering Corps to commemorate the occasion.

The guests began to arrive as the great clock in the Northern Tower struck the seventh hour.

They came in order of precedence, as tradition demanded. First the minor guilds: the Copper-Smiths and the Cable-Layers, the Insulator-Crafters and the Relay-Testers. These were the dwarves who had performed the physical labor of construction, who had strung miles of wire through dark tunnels and bolted relay housings to stone walls and tested every connection until their fingers ached. They took their places at the long tables that filled the lower portions of the hall, accepting cups of ale from servants and settling into conversation with the easy familiarity of colleagues who had worked side by side for years.

Next came the technical guilds: the Algorithm-Smiths and the Data-Architects, the Protocol-Designers and the System-Integrators. These were the dwarves who had transformed concepts into specifications and specifications into working code—or its Dwarven equivalent, the intricate mechanical calculators that processed the routing tables and managed the message queues. They occupied the middle tables, positioned where they could see both the common workers below and the nobility above, as befitted their status as bridges between labor and leadership.

Then came the Great Houses.

Lord Náin Fireforge entered with his family arrayed behind him in perfect formation, their boots striking the stone floor in unison. He was followed by Lady Dís, who had spent the past two days in the Third Deep and had emerged with seventeen pages of notes on potential improvements to the B-tree index structure. Their sons marched in order of age, each wearing the ceremonial armor of their house, each carrying a symbolic tool—a hammer for Thorin the Younger, a pair of tongs for Frerin, a measuring rod for young Dáin.

Lord Grór Ironfoot came next, still in his sedan chair but sitting upright now, his ancient eyes sweeping the hall with an expression that mixed pride with nostalgia. He was remembering, perhaps, the hall as it had looked in his youth, when the ceiling had not yet been raised and the pillars had numbered forty rather than one hundred and sixty. So much had changed. So much had been built. And yet the essential character of the place—the solidity, the permanence, the sense of endurance that was the hallmark of Dwarven craftsmanship—remained unchanged.

Lord Balin Goldvein made the most theatrical entrance, as was his custom. He had hired a troupe of musicians to precede him, playing a march that he had commissioned specifically for the occasion. The melody incorporated motifs from traditional mining songs, wedding hymns, and—in a touch that some found excessive—the rhythmic patterns of Morse code, spelling out the name "GOLDVEIN" in musical notation.

"Subtle as a cave-in," Thorin Stonehammer muttered to Kira Deepdelver, who had taken her place among the technical guilds. But he was smiling as he said it.

## Chapter VI

The King's entrance silenced every conversation in the hall.

Durin VI was not an imposing dwarf by physical standards. He stood barely four feet eight inches tall, and his beard, while respectably long, lacked the elaborate braiding that characterized the noble houses. He dressed simply, in robes of undyed wool that might have belonged to any common craftsman. He wore no crown, for the kings of Khazad-dûm had never favored such ornaments, preferring to let their deeds speak louder than any symbol.

But when Durin VI walked, the very stone seemed to recognize him. His footsteps echoed with an authority that transcended physical presence. His eyes, the color of granite flecked with gold, held the weight of forty generations of kings who had ruled from this hall. He moved with the deliberate pace of one who had never needed to hurry, who knew that time itself would wait upon his pleasure.

Behind him came the royal household: his queen, Dís the Wise, whose name was shared by many noble dwarves in her honor; his son and heir, Náin (not to be confused with Náin Fireforge, for the name was common among the nobility); and his counselors, seven dwarves of great age and greater wisdom who served as his advisors in matters of state.

The King mounted the dais and took his seat at the center of the high table. Only when he had settled into his chair did he speak, and his voice, though not loud, carried to every corner of the vast hall.

"Be seated, my people. This night, we are not king and subjects, lords and commons. We are Khazâd—the Dwarves—and we gather to celebrate what we have built together."

A rustle of movement as ten thousand dwarves took their seats. A moment of silence, pregnant with anticipation.

"Seventeen years ago," the King continued, "I stood in this very hall and approved a proposal that many thought impossible. A network of instantaneous communication, spanning every corner of our realm. The naysayers were many. The obstacles seemed insurmountable. And yet..."

He paused, reaching into his robe and withdrawing a small object: a telegraph key, its brass surface polished to a mirror shine.

"And yet here we are. The network is complete. I hold in my hand the very key that sent the first test message, preserved as a reminder of how far we have come."

He set the key on the table before him.

"This feast is to honor those who made it possible. Not merely the engineers and craftsmen, though their contributions were essential. Not merely the noble houses who funded the construction, though their generosity made the project feasible. But every dwarf who played a role, no matter how small. The miners who extracted the copper ore. The smelters who refined it into wire. The cooks who fed the construction crews. The healers who tended their injuries. The teachers who educated the next generation of engineers. All of you. This is your celebration."

A cheer rose from the assembled dwarves, starting in the lower tables and rippling upward until even the Great Houses joined in, their dignity momentarily forgotten in the collective joy of the moment.

The King raised his hand, and silence fell once more.

"But before we eat, before we drink, before we give ourselves over to merriment, I must speak of darker matters. For we do not build in isolation. Beyond our gates, Morgoth's shadow grows. His armies mass. His servants probe our defenses, seeking weakness."

The mood in the hall shifted. The joy remained, but it was tempered now with the awareness of threat, the knowledge that their celebration took place against a backdrop of war.

"The telegraph network is not merely a convenience," the King said. "It is a weapon. It allows us to coordinate our defenses as never before. An attack on the Northern Reaches can be reported to the Southern Galleries in moments. Reinforcements can be dispatched. Strategies can be coordinated. What once took weeks now takes seconds."

He stood, and his presence seemed to fill the hall.

"Let Morgoth come. Let his orcs and balrogs and nameless terrors throw themselves against our gates. They will find not scattered defenders but a unified realm, connected by bonds of copper and code, speaking with one voice, striking with one hammer. This is what we have built. This is what we celebrate tonight."

Another cheer, louder than before, with an edge of defiance that had been absent from the first.

"Now," the King said, his tone lightening, "let the feast begin!"

## Chapter VII

The food arrived in waves.

First came the breads: loaves of dense rye, studded with seeds and nuts, fresh from the great ovens of the Fourth Deep where heat from the forge-fires was channeled to feed a hundred bakeries. Beside the breads came butter churned from the milk of the great cave-cattle that grazed in the underground meadows, and preserves made from the pale fruits that grew in the phosphorescent orchards of the Southern Galleries.

Then came the meats: roasted boar from the forests beyond the gates, prepared in the traditional manner with a crust of wild herbs and honey; smoked fish from the underground lakes, their flesh pale and delicate; and the great delicacy of the Dwarven table, cave-beetle steak, marinated for three months in mushroom wine and so tender it could be cut with a spoon.

The vegetables followed: mushrooms of every variety, from tiny buttons to great slabs as large as platters; root vegetables that had never seen sunlight, their flesh sweet and dense; and salads of cave-moss dressed with vinegar and oil.

And throughout it all, the ale flowed.

Dwarven ale was legendary among the free peoples of Middle-earth. Brewed from grain that grew in the deep places where no sun shone, fermented in barrels carved from the heartwood of ancient trees that had been buried in the mountains since before the awakening of the Elves, it was simultaneously the smoothest and the strongest drink known to exist. A single mug could leave an Elf insensible for a week. A Man who attempted to match a Dwarf drink for drink would be carried home before the second course.

At the tables of the minor guilds, the ale inspired increasingly bold conversation. Old rivalries were revisited. Old triumphs were celebrated. Old grievances were, for the most part, set aside in favor of camaraderie.

"Do you remember," said a copper-smith named Borin to his tablemates, "when the relay at Junction Forty-Seven failed during the first system test? The whole Eastern Spur went dark. Took us three days to find the fault."

"A corroded contact," replied Lori, the cable-layer who had been his partner on that repair mission. "Three days of crawling through tunnels, and it came down to a speck of oxidation smaller than a grain of sand."

"That's when we started using gold contacts for critical junctions," Borin said. "Expensive, but worth it. Haven't had a corrosion failure since."

At the tables of the technical guilds, the conversation took a more abstract turn.

"The King spoke of the network as a weapon," said Farin, an algorithm-smith whose specialty was search optimization. "But I wonder if he fully appreciates its potential. The same infrastructure that carries military intelligence could carry trade information, market prices, weather reports from the surface. We've built a platform, not just a tool."

"Careful," warned his companion, a data-architect named Ori. "The last time someone proposed expanding the network's scope, the funding committee nearly collapsed from the strain of additional requirements."

"But think about it! Real-time inventory management across all seven Deeps. Automated reorder notifications when supplies run low. Predictive maintenance based on usage patterns. The PartsDB already has the data structures in place—it just needs the connections."

Ori shook her head, but she was smiling. "You're going to propose this to the King, aren't you?"

"Not tonight. Tonight, we celebrate. But tomorrow..."

At the high table, the conversation was more restrained but no less significant.

"The network performed flawlessly during the last orc incursion," Náin Fireforge was saying to the King. "The Northern watchtowers reported the enemy's approach, and our response was coordinated within the hour. In the old days, by the time word reached the main garrison, the battle would have been half over."

"And our casualties were the lightest in a century," the King replied. "Twenty-three wounded, none killed. The orcs lost six hundred. They will not forget that lesson quickly."

"They will adapt," said Grór Ironfoot, his voice creaking with age but his mind still sharp. "Morgoth's servants are not fools. They will find ways to disrupt our communications—cutting cables, destroying relay stations, perhaps even learning to send false messages."

"We've considered this," Kira Deepdelver interjected, having been invited to join the high table for her contributions to the project. "The network has redundant pathways. Cut one cable, and the traffic routes around the break. Destroy one relay, and the neighboring stations compensate. As for false messages..."

She hesitated, aware that she was about to reveal information that had been closely guarded.

The King nodded, giving her permission to continue.

"We've implemented a system of authentication codes. Each station has a unique signature, a sequence of patterns that cannot be forged without knowledge of the encoding algorithm. Any message that lacks proper authentication is flagged as suspicious and verified through alternate channels before being acted upon."

"And the encoding algorithm?" Grór asked. "How secure is it?"

"It's based on properties of large prime numbers," Kira said. "Even with all the computational power of Morgoth's servants, breaking the code would take longer than the remaining life of the sun."

A bold claim, but Kira had done the mathematics herself. She knew it to be true.

## Chapter VIII

As the feast progressed and the hour grew late, the formal structure began to dissolve. Dwarves moved from table to table, greeting old friends and making new acquaintances. The musicians who had accompanied Lord Balin Goldvein were joined by others, forming an impromptu orchestra that filled the hall with melodies both ancient and new.

In one corner of the hall, a group of young engineers had gathered around Kira Deepdelver, peppering her with questions about the network's design.

"How do you handle message congestion?" one asked. "During peak hours, the traffic must be enormous."

"Priority queuing," Kira explained. "Military and emergency messages go first, followed by government communications, then commercial, then personal. Within each category, messages are processed in the order received. The queues are implemented as binary heaps, so insertion and extraction are both O(log n)."

"But what if a high-priority message arrives when the queue is full of lower-priority traffic?"

"We use a variant of the aging algorithm. Messages that have been waiting too long are gradually promoted in priority, preventing starvation. But truly urgent messages—those marked with the royal seal—bypass the queue entirely and are transmitted immediately, preempting whatever else is in progress."

Another young engineer, barely more than an apprentice, raised his hand tentatively. "I've heard rumors about a project called the Palantír Protocol. Something about seeing stones and real-time communication. Is that... is that real?"

Kira smiled. The Palantír Protocol was indeed real, though its existence was not widely known outside the engineering corps. It was an extension of the telegraph network that allowed not just text messages but images—crude, low-resolution images composed of dots and dashes, but images nonetheless—to be transmitted between stations. The potential applications were staggering: maps could be shared instantly, diagrams of enemy positions could be sent from forward observers to command posts, even portraits could be transmitted to confirm the identity of messengers.

But the technology was still in its early stages, and the King had deemed it prudent to keep it secret until it was fully mature.

"Perhaps," Kira said carefully, "that's a conversation for another time."

The young engineer nodded, understanding the unspoken message. Some secrets were not meant to be discussed at feasts, no matter how much ale had been consumed.

Across the hall, a different kind of conversation was taking place.

Thorin Stonehammer had been cornered by Lady Dís Fireforge, who was explaining—with considerable enthusiasm and extensive hand gestures—her proposed modifications to the PartsDB indexing system.

"The current B-tree implementation is adequate," she was saying, "but it doesn't account for temporal patterns in access. Most queries are for parts that have been accessed recently—the principle of locality. If we add a caching layer based on a least-recently-used eviction policy, we could reduce average query time by thirty percent."

"Thirty percent is a significant improvement," Thorin admitted. "But it adds complexity. Another system to maintain, another point of potential failure."

"The complexity is manageable. I've worked out the implementation in detail." She produced a scroll from somewhere within her robes—Thorin had no idea where she had been keeping it—and unfurled it to reveal dense lines of pseudocode and diagrams. "The cache sits between the query interface and the main index. Hits are served immediately; misses fall through to the B-tree lookup and populate the cache on return. The eviction logic is a simple doubly-linked list with O(1) insertion and removal."

Thorin studied the scroll, his experienced eye picking out the key structures. It was elegant work. The integration points were clean, the error handling was comprehensive, and the performance analysis—occupying the final third of the scroll—was rigorous enough to satisfy even the most skeptical reviewer.

"You've thought this through," he said finally.

"I've been thinking about it for six months. Since the last time I visited the Third Deep and noticed the query latencies were higher than they should be."

"And you waited until tonight to propose it?"

Lady Dís shrugged. "The feast seemed like an appropriate occasion. A celebration of what we've built, and a reminder that there's always more building to be done."

Thorin rolled up the scroll and tucked it into his own robe. "I'll review it properly tomorrow, when my head is clearer. But provisionally... I think you may have earned yourself a implementation contract."

Lady Dís's smile was the smile of a dwarf who had gotten exactly what she wanted.

## Chapter IX

The night deepened.

The great clock struck the tenth hour, then the eleventh, then the twelfth. The lanterns that lined the walls burned lower, their light taking on the amber glow of aging flames. The food had long since been cleared away, replaced by platters of nuts and dried fruits and the sweet honeycakes that were traditional at Dwarven celebrations. The ale continued to flow, though many had switched to the lighter mushroom wine or the clear mountain water that was drawn from springs deep within the rock.

At the high table, the King had fallen into conversation with his oldest counselor, a dwarf named Fundin whose white beard brushed the floor and whose memories stretched back to the reign of Durin IV.

"I remember when this hall was built," Fundin was saying, his voice barely above a whisper yet somehow carrying with perfect clarity. "I was young then—not yet forty. They assigned me to carry stone blocks from the quarry to the construction site. Back and forth, all day long, for years on end."

"You've seen much change," the King observed.

"Change beyond imagining. When I was young, a message from the Eastern Deeps was a week's journey. Now it's the work of a moment. When I was young, the PartsDB didn't exist—every forge kept its own records, in its own format, and finding a specific component could take days of searching. Now..." He shook his head in wonderment. "Now you type a query and the answer appears like magic."

"Not magic," the King said gently. "Engineering. The work of minds and hands, not arcane power."

"Perhaps. But to one who remembers the old ways, it might as well be magic." Fundin was silent for a moment, his ancient eyes losing focus as memory carried him to distant times. "I wonder sometimes what the founders would think if they could see what we've built. Durin the Deathless, who carved the first halls. Would he be proud? Or would he think we've strayed too far from the simple virtues of stone and steel?"

It was a question the King had asked himself many times.

"I believe," he said slowly, "that Durin would recognize the essential Dwarvenness of what we do. We build. We improve. We endure. The tools change—from hammer and chisel to telegraph and algorithm—but the spirit remains the same. We take the raw material of the world and shape it to our purposes. We solve problems. We create order from chaos."

"And we gather to celebrate when the work is done," Fundin added, a trace of his old humor returning to his voice.

"And we gather to celebrate," the King agreed. "For what is the point of building, if not to share in the joy of completion?"

They sat in companionable silence for a moment, watching the younger dwarves move through the hall, their laughter echoing off the ancient stones.

"There will be harder days ahead," Fundin said eventually. "Morgoth's shadow grows. War is coming—not the skirmishes we've known, but true war, the kind that shapes the fate of ages."

"I know."

"The network you've built will be tested. Everything we've built will be tested."

"I know."

"And yet you chose to hold this feast. To celebrate, when others might have urged preparation for battle."

The King nodded. "Because this is what we fight for. Not just survival, but the right to build, to create, to gather in halls of our own making and share in the fruits of our labor. If we lose sight of that—if we become nothing more than warriors, defined by our enemies rather than our achievements—then Morgoth has already won."

Fundin considered this for a long moment.

"Durin would be proud," he said at last. "The founders would be proud. We have built well, my King. Whatever comes next, we have built well."
