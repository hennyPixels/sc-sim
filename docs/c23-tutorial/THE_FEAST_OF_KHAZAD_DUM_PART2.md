# The Feast of Khazad-dûm

## A Chronicle of the Ages

### Part II: The Songs of the Deeps

*Being an Account of the Tales Told and Songs Sung during the Great Feast,
wherein the Labors of the Common Folk are Honored and the Ancient
Traditions of the Khazâd are Remembered*

---

# Book Two: The Working Folk

## Chapter X

As the formal feast gave way to the deeper hours of the night, the character of the celebration shifted. The nobles and the guilds-masters had said their pieces; now it was time for the common folk to have their say.

This was an ancient tradition among the Dwarves, dating back to the earliest days of Khazad-dûm when the distinction between king and commoner had been little more than a matter of who held the heaviest hammer. In those days, any dwarf could stand before the assembly and speak—of grievances, of achievements, of dreams for the future. The tradition had evolved over the millennia, becoming more structured and ceremonial, but its essence remained: the voice of the worker was honored alongside the voice of the lord.

The first to rise was not a young dwarf eager for recognition, but an old one—older even than Fundin, older perhaps than any other in the hall save for Lord Grór Ironfoot himself. His name was Bofur, and he had worked the cable-laying crews for three hundred and twelve years.

He stood slowly, his joints creaking in protest, and made his way to the speaking stone at the center of the hall—a raised platform of polished granite that had witnessed ten thousand speeches over the centuries. The crowd quieted as he climbed the three steps to the top, and when he spoke, his voice was rough with age but clear enough to reach the farthest corners of the room.

"Most of you don't know me," he began. "I'm not a lord. I'm not an engineer. I've never designed an algorithm or balanced a tree or optimized a query. For three hundred years, my job has been simple: take a spool of copper wire, carry it through a tunnel, and spool it out along the wall while my partner bolts it in place. That's it. That's what I've done, every day, for longer than most of you have been alive."

He paused, letting the words settle.

"Some might say that's not important work. Anyone can carry a spool. Anyone can walk through a tunnel. But I want to tell you something—when the telegraph system was finished, when that first message went from the Southern Galleries to the Northern Reaches, I was there. I was standing in Junction Forty-Seven, the junction where Borin found the corroded contact he was talking about earlier. And I knew—I knew—that the signal passing through that junction was traveling along wire that I had carried. Wire that I had unspooled with my own hands. Wire that I had watched being bolted into place, meter by meter, year by year."

His voice grew stronger as he spoke, the passion of a lifetime's work animating his aged frame.

"The engineers designed the system. The lords funded it. The King blessed it. But I carried the wire. Me and Lori and Borin and a thousand others whose names you'll never know. We're the ones who made it real. We're the ones who turned drawings into copper and copper into communication. And tonight, I want to say this: thank you. Thank you for remembering us. Thank you for inviting us to this feast. Thank you for letting an old cable-layer stand on the speaking stone and have his say."

He raised his mug—ale, still half-full despite the hour—and the crowd rose as one, raising their own mugs in response.

"To the telegraph!" Bofur shouted. "And to everyone who built it!"

"To the telegraph!" the crowd roared back, and the sound of ten thousand voices speaking as one shook dust from the ancient rafters.

---

## Chapter XI

After Bofur, others came forward to speak.

There was Nori, a young dwarf-woman who had worked as a signal tester during the final phases of construction. Her job had been to sit at relay stations and tap out test patterns, hour after hour, listening for distortions or delays that might indicate a fault in the line. It was tedious work, mind-numbing in its repetition, and yet essential—a single undetected fault could cascade through the network, corrupting messages and undermining the entire system.

"They gave me a manual when I started," Nori told the crowd. "It said the standard test pattern was: dot, dash, dot, dot, dash. Simple enough. But the manual was written by engineers, and engineers don't always think about what it's like to sit in a dark relay station, alone, tapping out the same pattern for eight hours straight."

Laughter rippled through the crowd. Many had performed similar duties during their apprenticeships.

"After about six months, I started going a little crazy. So I started making up my own test patterns. Dot, dot, dot, dot, dash, dash, dash, dash. That's the letter H followed by the number 8 in Morse. H-8. Get it? I hated my job."

More laughter, louder now.

"But then something happened. I was testing Junction Nineteen—deep in the Western Mines, so far from anywhere that the nearest other dwarf was half a mile away. And I heard something. Not my test pattern. Something else. A message, coming through the line from somewhere far away. It was garbled, broken up, but I could make out bits of it. 'Need... help... collapse...'"

The laughter died. The crowd leaned forward.

"There had been a cave-in. Not a big one—the Western Mines are stable—but enough to trap a work crew in a side tunnel. They had a telegraph key, and they were using it to call for help. But the main lines weren't active yet. The only reason I heard them was because I was testing Junction Nineteen, and their distress call was being carried by one of the backup circuits we hadn't even documented properly."

She paused, remembering.

"I didn't know what to do. The manual didn't cover this. So I did the only thing I could think of: I started tapping back. Dot, dash, dot. R. R for 'received.' I told them help was coming. I told them to stay calm. And then I ran—ran all the way to the nearest manned station, half a mile through dark tunnels, and sounded the alarm."

"They got there in time. The rescue crews dug out the trapped workers, all twelve of them, without a single casualty. And afterward, the chief engineer came to see me. He said, 'You saved their lives.' And I said, 'No. The telegraph saved their lives. I just happened to be listening.'"

She raised her mug. "To the telegraph. And to everyone who listens."

---

## Chapter XII

The hours passed, and the stories continued.

Each tale added another layer to the tapestry of achievement that the feast was meant to celebrate. There was the story of the generator failure in the Second Deep, when a critical hydroelectric turbine had seized and the power grid had threatened to collapse—until a quick-thinking engineer had rerouted power through an unused industrial circuit, maintaining the network's integrity until repairs could be made. There was the story of the surveying expedition that had discovered a vast new cavern system to the south, a discovery that had been immediately reported via telegraph, allowing planning to begin while the surveyors were still mapping the terrain.

But not all the stories were triumphant. Some spoke of loss.

Old Bifur, who had lost his son in a construction accident fifteen years earlier, stood at the speaking stone and spoke of sacrifice.

"My boy Bofur—not the cable-layer, another Bofur—he was an insulator-crafter. Specialized in the ceramic housings that keep the wires from touching the rock. Delicate work. Dangerous work. One day, a housing cracked during installation. The wire touched the wall. There was a spark, and my boy..."

He stopped, overcome by memory.

The crowd waited in respectful silence.

"My boy didn't make it," Bifur continued, his voice thick. "But the section he was working on—the section that killed him—that section is still in use today. Every message that passes through the Eastern Spur, every telegram that connects the Fireforge estate to the main network, travels through wire that my boy helped install. In a way, he's still there. Still doing his job. Still part of the network."

He raised his mug, and his hand trembled only slightly.

"To those we've lost. To those who gave their lives so that we could gather here tonight. May their names be remembered as long as the mountains endure."

"To those we've lost," the crowd echoed, and for a moment the celebration was touched by solemnity, by the recognition that great achievements always come at great cost.

---

## Chapter XIII

It was past the third hour of the morning when the storytelling gave way to song.

This too was tradition. At Dwarven feasts, songs served multiple purposes: they preserved history, they built camaraderie, they allowed dwarves who might never speak in public to participate in the collective expression of joy or sorrow. The songs of Khazad-dûm were famous throughout Middle-earth—deep, resonant melodies that seemed to arise from the stone itself, harmonies that echoed the mathematical precision of Dwarven architecture.

The first song was ancient, older than the telegraph or the PartsDB or any of the modern marvels being celebrated. It was called "The Founding of the Mansion," and its verses recounted the discovery of the caverns that would become Khazad-dûm, the first settlements, the gradual expansion that had transformed a natural formation into the greatest underground realm in history.

*In the depths where no sun ever shone,*
*Where water carved paths through the living stone,*
*Durin awoke by the mirror-mere,*
*And saw in its surface a crown appear.*

*He called to his kindred: "Come, follow me,*
*To halls of wonder that soon shall be,*
*Where hammer and chisel shall write our song,*
*And mountains shall echo our name for long."*

The melody was simple—a steady march in a minor key—but when ten thousand voices joined in unison, the effect was overwhelming. The harmonics built upon each other, creating overtones that seemed to resonate with the very structure of the hall. Young dwarves who had never heard the song before found themselves singing along, the melody seemingly encoded in their blood.

When "The Founding" concluded, a new song began—newer but no less beloved. This was "The Forging of Mithril," which told the story of the first discovery of the precious metal that had made Khazad-dûm wealthy beyond measure.

*Deep delved the miners, seeking gold and gems,*
*But found instead a silver light,*
*That shone like stars upon their helms,*
*And drove away the endless night.*

*"What is this wonder?" cried the King,*
*"That glows as if with inner fire?"*
*The smiths came forth, began to sing,*
*And forged it on the sacred pyre.*

*Mithril! Mithril! Stronger than steel,*
*Lighter than silk upon the skin,*
*Mithril! Mithril! Let Morgoth's hordes reel,*
*When Dwarven mail their blades strike thin!*

This song was more energetic, with a driving rhythm that set feet to stamping and hands to clapping. By the final chorus, the entire hall was in motion, a sea of dwarves swaying and stomping in time to the music. The very floor seemed to vibrate with their enthusiasm.

---

## Chapter XIV

After the traditional songs came songs of more recent composition—ballads that celebrated the achievements of the current age, set to melodies that borrowed from the old while adding new flourishes.

The most popular of these was "The Song of the Singing Wire," composed only five years earlier by a young bard named Dori. It told the story of the telegraph network in the language of epic poetry, transforming technical achievements into legendary deeds.

*Hear now the tale of the wire that sings,*
*Of mountains connected by copper strings,*
*Of messages flying on electric wings,*
*And the crafters who fashioned these wonderful things.*

*In ages of old, when a word must needs travel,*
*Through tunnels of stone that would sooner unravel,*
*A runner would carry it, step after step,*
*While senders grew weary, and promises slept.*

*But then came the dreamers, the makers, the wise,*
*Who looked at the mountains with wondering eyes,*
*And said: "We shall build what has never been built,*
*A network of song that shall never be stilt!"*

The song had seventeen verses, each celebrating a different aspect of the network's construction. There were verses for the surveyors who mapped the routes, the miners who carved the conduit tunnels, the smiths who refined the copper wire, the engineers who designed the relay stations, and a dozen other specialties besides. By the end, every guild represented in the hall had been honored, and the sense of collective achievement was palpable.

But it was the final verse that brought tears to many eyes:

*Now the wire sings ever, from dawn until dawn,*
*Connecting the fathers and mothers and spawn,*
*Of mountain and valley, of depth and of height,*
*United as one in the song of the night.*

*So remember the crafters who gave us this gift,*
*Who labored through years that seemed longer than swift,*
*And know that their voices, though silent they be,*
*Still echo forever in telegraphry.*

The crowd cheered when the song concluded, and Dori herself—a slight dwarf with an unusually long beard that she wore braided with tiny golden beads—was called to the speaking stone to receive the acclaim of her peers.

"I didn't write this song alone," she said, blushing beneath her beard. "I interviewed dozens of crafters, listened to their stories, tried to capture what they felt in words and music. The song belongs to all of us."

---

## Chapter XV

As the night deepened toward its darkest hour, the songs took on a different character. The triumphant anthems gave way to older, stranger melodies—songs that spoke of mystery and wonder, of the deep places of the earth where even Dwarves rarely ventured.

These were the Songs of the Deeps, passed down from generation to generation in an oral tradition that predated written language itself. They spoke of things that the rational, engineering-minded dwarves of the modern age preferred not to discuss openly: the spirits that were said to dwell in the deepest caverns, the strange lights that sometimes appeared in unexplored tunnels, the feeling—familiar to any miner who had worked alone—of being watched by unseen eyes.

The most famous of these was "The Dweller in Darkness," a ballad that told of an encounter between a lone miner and... something. The song never specified what the miner had met in the depths. It spoke only of a presence, a vast intelligence that had communicated without words, that had seemed to look through the miner's soul and find it worthy—or at least not unworthy—of passage.

*I delved too deep, I dug too far,*
*Below the reach of lamp or star,*
*And there I met what has no name,*
*A darkness darker than the same.*

*It did not speak, it did not move,*
*Yet somehow I could feel it prove,*
*The measure of my Dwarven heart,*
*And judge if I should stay or part.*

*I stood my ground, I did not flee,*
*Though terror gripped the soul of me,*
*And in that moment, understood,*
*That some things dwell where none should.*

*It let me pass, it let me go,*
*Back to the world of friends and foe,*
*But in my dreams I sometimes see,*
*That darkness looking back at me.*

The song was sung quietly, almost in a whisper, and when it ended, no one cheered. Instead, the crowd sat in contemplative silence, each dwarf lost in private thoughts.

It was Grór Ironfoot who broke the spell.

"I met the Dweller once," he said, his ancient voice carrying through the hall. "In my youth, when I was foolish enough to explore beyond the mapped tunnels. I never told anyone. I thought they wouldn't believe me. But the song speaks true. There are things in the depths that we do not understand. Things that have been there since before our people awakened. Things that may still be there when our people are gone."

He paused, his rheumy eyes scanning the crowd.

"This is why we build. Not to conquer the mountain, but to carve out a space where we can live in safety, in light, in the company of our kindred. The telegraph network is not just a tool for communication. It is a lifeline. A reminder that we are not alone in the darkness. That help is always within reach."

The King nodded slowly. "Well said, Lord Ironfoot. Well said."

---

## Chapter XVI

The songs of mystery gave way, eventually, to songs of a more intimate nature: love songs, family songs, songs of home and hearth.

These were not performed for the whole hall, but in small groups gathered at tables or in corners of the vast chamber. Old married couples sang duets they had composed for each other decades earlier. Parents sang lullabies to children who had long since grown to adulthood but who still remembered the melodies from their cradles. Friends sang the drinking songs they had shared in their youth, their voices rough with age but their spirits undiminished.

At one table, Thorin Stonehammer found himself singing a song he hadn't thought of in a century—a simple tune his mother had sung to him as an infant, about a little stone that dreamed of becoming a mountain. The words came back to him unbidden, and he was surprised to find tears in his eyes as he sang.

*Little stone, little stone,*
*Lying by the road alone,*
*What do you dream in the dark of night?*
*I dream of growing to a mountain's height.*

*Little stone, little stone,*
*Seed of something yet unknown,*
*Will you reach your mountain's crown?*
*Only if I don't stay down.*

It was a children's song, silly and simple, but it spoke to something fundamental in the Dwarven character: the belief that even the smallest beginning could lead to the greatest achievement, if only one had the patience and perseverance to continue.

Nearby, Kira Deepdelver was listening to a group of young engineers sing a parody song they had composed during the long nights of the network's construction. It was called "The Algorithm Blues," and its verses catalogued, with dark humor, all the ways a well-designed system could go catastrophically wrong.

*My hash function had a collision,*
*My B-tree lost its precision,*
*My queue overflowed at the worst possible time,*
*And debugging this mess is a serious crime.*

*Oh, I've got the algorithm blues,*
*Got bugs in my boots and bugs in my shoes,*
*The stack trace is fifty levels deep,*
*And I haven't had a moment of sleep!*

Kira laughed despite herself. She remembered those nights—the frustration, the exhaustion, the moment of despair when it seemed the project would never be completed. But she also remembered the triumphs: the first successful transmission, the first day of full network operation, the gradual realization that they had accomplished something unprecedented.

"You should add a verse about the relay timing problem," she suggested. "The one I solved with predictive compensation."

The engineers exchanged glances.

"Chief Architect," one of them said hesitantly, "we... we already have a verse about that. We just didn't want to sing it in front of you."

"Oh?" Kira raised an eyebrow. "Let me hear it."

The engineers shuffled uncomfortably, but finally one of them—the youngest, who perhaps had the least to lose—began to sing:

*Kira came down from the top of the chart,*
*Said "Your relays are breaking the system apart,"*
*She solved it with math that we couldn't believe,*
*And made us all feel like we'd rolled up our sleeve.*

*Oh, we've got the algorithm blues,*
*But Kira showed us what we had to lose,*
*The timing compensation saved the day,*
*Now signals travel faster all the way!*

Kira blinked. Then she laughed—a genuine, unrestrained laugh that seemed to surprise even herself.

"That's actually quite good," she said. "You should add more verses about other people's contributions. Make it a team song."

The young engineers nodded eagerly, and Kira could see them already composing additional verses in their heads.

---

## Chapter XVII

By the fourth hour of the morning, the energy in the hall had shifted once again. The songs had grown quieter, more reflective. Many of the older dwarves had retired to their quarters, their stamina finally exhausted. The younger ones remained, clustered in small groups, speaking in low voices of the future.

At one such group, a debate was underway about the next great project.

"The network is complete," argued a dwarf named Fíli, "but it's just the beginning. Imagine if we could transmit not just text, but images. Maps. Diagrams. The Palantír Protocol shows it's possible—why not extend it to the entire network?"

"The bandwidth isn't there," countered his companion, Kíli. "The current infrastructure can barely handle the text traffic during peak hours. Images would require ten times the capacity, maybe more."

"Then we build more capacity. More cables. More relay stations. More generators."

"And who pays for all this? The Lords have already spent a fortune on the current network. Convincing them to fund an expansion—"

"The expansion will pay for itself! Think of the applications. Remote medical diagnosis—a doctor in the First Deep could examine a patient in the Southern Galleries, based on transmitted images of their symptoms. Architectural planning—blueprints could be shared instantly across the realm. Military intelligence—actual photographs of enemy positions, rather than written descriptions that might be misinterpreted."

The debate continued, voices rising and falling, points made and countered. Kira, who had drifted over to listen, smiled to herself. These young dwarves reminded her of herself, a century ago, when she had first begun to dream of what the telegraph network could become.

She didn't interrupt. Let them dream. Let them argue. Let them discover for themselves what was possible and what was merely fantasy. That was how progress was made—not by a single genius working in isolation, but by the collective effort of many minds, each contributing their piece of the puzzle.

---

## Chapter XVIII

The fifth hour came and went. The sixth hour approached.

In the Eastern Deeps, dawn would be breaking over the world outside—a world that most of the dwarves in the hall had never seen and had no desire to see. They were a people of the mountain, creatures of stone and darkness, and the sun held no appeal for them.

But even in the darkness of Khazad-dûm, there was a rhythm to the day. The forges would be firing up soon. The mines would resume their ceaseless extraction. The telegraph network would carry its first messages of the morning—reports of overnight incidents, requests for supplies, the mundane communications that kept the realm functioning.

The King rose from his seat, and the hall fell silent.

"The night grows old," he said, "and even Dwarves must rest. But before we part, I wish to speak one final time of what we have accomplished and what lies ahead."

He descended from the high table and walked among the remaining guests—nobles and commoners alike, old and young, the great and the humble. They parted before him, forming a corridor of flesh through which he passed like a current through stone.

"We have built a marvel," he continued. "The telegraph network connects every corner of our realm. The PartsDB tracks every component of our vast enterprise. The power grid ensures that the lights never dim and the machines never stop. These are achievements worthy of song, worthy of celebration, worthy of this feast."

He stopped walking. He had reached the speaking stone, and he mounted it with the grace of one who had climbed these steps a thousand times.

"But we cannot rest on our achievements. Morgoth's shadow grows. His armies mass beyond the gates. His spies probe our defenses, seeking weakness. We have built tools that will help us resist him—but tools alone are not enough."

His gaze swept the hall, meeting the eyes of every dwarf who remained.

"What we need is what we have always needed: courage, determination, and the unshakeable conviction that our cause is just. We are not merely defending territory. We are defending a way of life—the life of builders and makers, of craftsmen and artists, of families and communities that have endured for millennia. This is what Morgoth seeks to destroy. This is what we must protect."

He raised his hand, and an attendant brought forth a small wooden box, ornately carved with the symbols of the Seven Houses.

"In this box," the King said, "are seven keys. Each is identical to the key I showed you earlier—the key that sent the first test message through the completed network. One key will be given to each of the Great Houses, as a symbol of our unity and a reminder of what we have built together."

He opened the box and began to distribute the keys. Lord Náin Fireforge received his with a deep bow. Lord Grór Ironfoot accepted his from his sedan chair, clutching it in trembling hands. Lord Balin Goldvein received his with tears streaming unashamedly down his plump cheeks.

"These keys are more than symbols," the King concluded. "They are functional. In times of crisis, they can be used to send priority messages that will supersede all other traffic on the network. Use them wisely. Use them sparingly. But when the need arises, use them without hesitation."

He stepped down from the speaking stone.

"The feast is ended. Go to your rest. And tomorrow, we continue to build."
